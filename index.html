Why social and emotional development matters

Social-emotional growth starts before birth and continues through adolescence. Teachers can support that growth when we understand development and give students chances to practice these skills every day.

Early Development & Environmental Influences

Prenatal care and early experiences can affect later coping, relationships, and emotional development.

Social-Emotional Milestones

Children's needs change as they grow. Milestones help educators choose age-appropriate support.

Early Development & Lasting Impact

What happens early can influence how students later respond to stress, relationships, learning, and emotional challenges.

ABC★✏1+2

Prenatal Development & Care

Nutrition, medical care, maternal health, exposure to substances, and severe or prolonged stress can influence the developing brain and body. Early risk does not guarantee later difficulty, but it can increase vulnerability when several risks occur together.

(Shonkoff et al., 2012; Ormrod et al., 2024)

Early Childhood Experiences

Responsive adults, safe routines, play, language, and support during strong emotions help children develop trust and coping skills. Chronic adversity without enough adult support can make stress regulation and learning more difficult.

(Shonkoff et al., 2012)

How It Can Show Up in Adolescence

Earlier experiences may be reflected in coping, trust, self-concept, stress reactions, decision-making, and relationships. Supportive adults and direct teaching of coping and problem-solving skills can still strengthen development.

(Ormrod et al., 2024; Shonkoff et al., 2012)

Early experiences matter, but they are not destiny. Positive relationships and support can continue to make a difference throughout childhood and adolescence.

Social-Emotional Milestones

Three stages, three different sets of needs—and many chances for teachers to help students grow.

Developmental stages

Early childhood, middle childhood, and adolescence

Milestones are general guides, not deadlines. Culture, language, temperament, disability, family experiences, and opportunities for practice can affect how development looks.

Early Childhood • 3–5

Feelings, play, and early friendship skills

  • Names basic emotions and begins talking about feelings.
  • Shows growing empathy and concern for others.
  • Practices sharing, turn-taking, and cooperative play.
  • Begins managing impulses with adult help.
Middle Childhood • 6–11

Friendship, confidence, and problem-solving

  • Peer relationships become more important.
  • Better understands another person's point of view.
  • Uses more complex ways to solve disagreements.
  • Develops a stronger sense of strengths and weaknesses.
Adolescence • 12–18

Identity, independence, and deeper relationships

  • Explores identity, beliefs, values, and future goals.
  • Seeks more independence while peers become highly important.
  • Handles more complex social situations and perspectives.
  • Continues developing emotional regulation and decision-making.

Developmental framing: Ormrod et al. (2024).

Classroom Strategies

SEL works best when students practice the skills during real classroom routines—not only after a problem happens.

✏️📚🍎⭐
Early Childhood
1. Feelings check-in + story talkStudents select an emotion picture at arrival. During read-alouds, ask how a character feels and what might help.
2. Practice social language through playModel phrases such as “Can I have a turn?” “I need help,” and “Let's try again.” Practice before a conflict happens.
Middle Childhood
1. Rotate group rolesUse facilitator, recorder, materials manager, and reporter so students practice leading, listening, contributing, and supporting others.
2. Reflect after group workAsk: “What happened?” “How did it affect others?” and “What could we try next time?”
Adolescence
1. Private check-in + goal settingStudents privately rate stress/readiness and set one realistic goal. Follow up without putting them on the spot.
2. Real-life social scenariosDiscuss peer pressure, online conflict, relationships, deadlines, asking for help, and possible consequences.
Self-regulation

Self-regulation is more than “behaving.”

Slater (2021) explains self-regulation as recognizing brain-body responses to stress and using strategies to return to a calmer, more manageable state.

1. PAUSEStop long enough to notice what is happening.
2. NOTICEName the feeling, body signal, or trigger.
3. CHOOSEUse a strategy that helps you regulate and return to learning.

Behavior can be communication.

What looks like defiance, refusal, or “bad behavior” may be a student who is overwhelmed and does not yet have the regulation skill needed for that moment. Support does not remove accountability; it teaches the student what to do next time.

(Slater, 2021)

🗣️
Model it

Talk through your own calm problem-solving and coping process.

🤝
Co-regulate

Use a calm voice, less language, space, and simple choices before discussing consequences.

🧠
Practice early

Teach breathing, break requests, movement, and calming choices before the hard moment.

Families Are Part of the Team

Everyday conversations and routines at home can reinforce the same social-emotional skills children practice at school.

Early Childhood

Name feelings: “You look disappointed because playtime ended.” Offer simple calming choices such as a hug, quiet minute, or breathing together.

Practice: Keep routines predictable and use play, cooking, and simple games to practice waiting and turn-taking.

Middle Childhood

Check in: Ask, “What was easy today? What was hard? What do you need tomorrow?” Help the child think of a next step instead of solving everything for them.

Practice: Break homework into parts, use movement breaks, and make a short coping-choice list together.

Adolescence

Stay connected: Ask open questions, listen before giving advice, and respect growing independence while staying involved.

Practice: Work together on routines for sleep, schoolwork, responsibilities, stress, and screen use. Normalize asking trusted adults for help.

Adolescent safety matters

Withdrawal, major changes in sleep or eating, a sudden drop in functioning, self-harm, hopelessness, or talk about wanting to die should be taken seriously. Educators should follow school safety procedures and connect the student with qualified support rather than trying to manage a crisis alone.

U.S. crisis support: Call or text 988. For a life-threatening emergency, call 911.
